# Rhetorical Devices

Ten classical rhetorical techniques dating back to ancient Greece and Rome for crafting persuasive, memorable communication.

## When to use

- Formal speeches and presentations
- Executive communications and proposals
- Professional writing that needs impact
- Persuasive arguments and pitches
- Important emails to stakeholders

## When NOT to use

- Casual messages or Slack chats
- Informal emails to colleagues
- Quick status updates
- Everyday conversation

## The golden rule

**Use sparingly.** One or two well-placed devices create impact. Stacking multiple devices sounds overwrought and manipulative. Choose the right tool for the moment.

## The 10 Devices

### 1. Tricolon (Rule of Three)

Group ideas in threes for memorability and rhythm.

**Pattern:** A, B, and C

**Examples:**
> "A rupture in the world order, the end of a pleasant fiction, and the beginning of a harsh reality."

> "Government of the people, by the people, for the people." — Abraham Lincoln

> "Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness." — US Declaration of Independence

**Why it works:** Three items feel complete. Fewer feels incomplete; more overwhelms. People remember, believe, and act on things stated in threes.

---

### 2. Antithesis (Contrast)

Place opposing ideas side by side to highlight differences.

**Pattern:** [positive/strong] vs [negative/weak]

**Examples:**
> "The strong can do what they can; the weak must suffer what they must."

> "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." — Neil Armstrong

> "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times." — Charles Dickens

**Why it works:** Contrast focuses attention on injustice, difference, or choice by shining a spotlight on the gap.

---

### 3. Asyndeton (Connector Removal)

Remove conjunctions between words or phrases for urgency.

**Pattern:** A, B, C (no "and")

**Examples:**
> "No limits, no constraints."

> "I came, I saw, I conquered." — Julius Caesar (Veni, vidi, vici)

> "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields." — Winston Churchill

**Why it works:** The abruptness suggests breathlessness, anxiety, urgency. The pace quickens.

---

### 4. Polyptoton (Root Repetition)

Repeat the same root word in different forms.

**Pattern:** [word-form-1] of the [word-form-2]

**Examples:**
> "The Power of the Powerless" — Václav Havel

> "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself." — Franklin D. Roosevelt

> "Who will police the police?" (Quis custodiet ipsos custodes?)

**Why it works:** The repetition highlights paradox, tension, and irony while creating a memorable phrase.

---

### 5. Analogy

Compare an unfamiliar situation to a familiar one.

**Pattern:** X is like Y (where Y is well-understood and emotionally resonant)

**Examples:**
> Soviet shopkeepers displaying Marxist slogans they didn't believe in → world leaders defending rules they don't follow

> "A good speech should be like a woman's skirt: long enough to cover the subject and short enough to create interest." — Winston Churchill

> Comparing a company's culture to a sports team: "You don't get to keep your spot just because you were here last season."

**Why it works:** Activates existing associations, instincts, and emotions. Makes abstract concepts concrete.

---

### 6. Hyperbole (Deliberate Exaggeration)

Use extreme language to emphasize a point.

**Pattern:** Amplify beyond literal truth for effect

**Examples:**
> Calling something "a lie, a fiction, false" rather than "inaccurate"

> "I've told you a million times not to exaggerate."

> "This isn't a setback. This is a catastrophe. This is an extinction-level event for our industry."

**Why it works:** Breaks through noise. Signals this matters. Use rarely or it loses power.

---

### 7. Antimetabole (Phrase Reversal)

Swap words from the first half of a sentence in the second half.

**Pattern:** A B → B A

**Examples:**
> "We're no longer relying on the strength of our values, but the value of our strength."

> "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country." — John F. Kennedy

> "It's not the size of the dog in the fight, it's the size of the fight in the dog." — Mark Twain

**Why it works:** The reversal sounds profound. The symmetry is satisfying. The contrast is sharp.

---

### 8. Metaphor

Speak of something as if it were something else.

**Pattern:** X is Y (not "X is like Y")

**Examples:**
> "If we're not at the table, we're on the menu."

> "All the world's a stage, and all the men and women merely players." — William Shakespeare

> "Time is money." — Benjamin Franklin

**Why it works:** Activates primitive instincts. "Humans as food" triggers survival responses. Makes abstract threats visceral.

---

### 9. Anaphora (Opening Repetition)

Repeat the same word or phrase at the start of successive clauses.

**Pattern:** "It means X. It means Y. It means Z."

**Examples:**
> "It means naming reality. It means acting consistently. It means building what we claim to believe in."

> "I have a dream that one day... I have a dream that one day... I have a dream that one day..." — Martin Luther King Jr.

> "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills; we shall never surrender." — Winston Churchill

**Why it works:** Builds rhythm and momentum. Creates emphasis through repetition. Sounds authoritative and statesmanlike.

---

### 10. Rhyme

End key phrases with rhyming words, especially for conclusions.

**Pattern:** ...ation / ...ation (or any rhyme)

**Examples:**
> "Integration becoming the source of subordination."

> "If it doesn't fit, you must acquit." — Johnnie Cochran (O.J. Simpson trial)

> "Fail to prepare, prepare to fail."

**Why it works:** Rhymes feel true (the "rhyme-as-reason" effect). Creates memorable slogans. Signals conclusion.

## Quick reference

| Device | Pattern | Effect |
|--------|---------|--------|
| Tricolon | A, B, C | Memorable, complete |
| Antithesis | X vs Y | Highlights contrast |
| Asyndeton | A, B, C (no "and") | Urgency |
| Polyptoton | power/powerless | Paradox, irony |
| Analogy | X is like Y | Makes abstract concrete |
| Hyperbole | Extreme language | Emphasis |
| Antimetabole | A B, B A | Profound reversal |
| Metaphor | X is Y | Visceral, instinctive |
| Anaphora | Repeat opening | Authority, momentum |
| Rhyme | Sound matching | Memorable, "feels true" |

## Usage guidance

When helping with formal writing or speeches:

1. **Identify the core message** - What one thing must the audience remember?
2. **Choose 1-2 devices max** - More dilutes impact
3. **Match device to purpose:**
   - Need memorability? → Tricolon, Rhyme
   - Need urgency? → Asyndeton
   - Need to highlight injustice? → Antithesis
   - Need a quotable line? → Antimetabole, Metaphor
   - Need to build to a climax? → Anaphora
4. **Place strategically** - Opening, key argument, or closing
5. **Read aloud** - Rhetoric is meant to be heard
